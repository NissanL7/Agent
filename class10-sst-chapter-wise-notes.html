✍️ Answer points — Unification of Germany (5M)
  1. In 1848 the middle classes of the German states tried to unite Germany through the Frankfurt Parliament (St. Paul's Church, 18 May 1848) — it was dominated by the middle classes and failed when the Prussian king Frederick Wilhelm IV rejected the crown offered to him.
  2. Germany was then unified by Prussia under its Chief Minister Otto von Bismarck (1862), who carried out the process with the help of the Prussian army and bureaucracy.
  3. Bismarck fought three wars over seven years — with Denmark (1864), Austria (1866) and France (1870–71) — and Prussia won all three.
  4. In January 1871, the Prussian king William I was proclaimed the German Emperor in the Hall of Mirrors at the Palace of Versailles.
  5. The new German empire gave primacy to Prussian measures and practices — the Prussian currency, banking system, army and bureaucracy dominated, and the Junker class retained political power.
✍️ Answer points — Unification of Italy (5M)
  1. Italy was politically fragmented into seven states, of which only Sardinia-Piedmont was ruled by an Italian princely house; the north was under Austrian Habsburgs, the centre under the Pope, and the south under the Bourbon kings of Spain.
  2. Giuseppe Mazzini (Genoa, 1831) formed the secret society Young Italy to make Italy a unitary republic; his uprisings of 1831 and 1848 failed, and the leadership then moved to the ruling class.
  3. Count Camillo di Cavour, Chief Minister of Sardinia-Piedmont from 1852, unified Italy through diplomacy and war — a tactical alliance with France (Napoleon III) helped defeat Austria in 1859.
  4. Giuseppe Garibaldi led the volunteer Red Shirts and in 1860 marched into South Italy and the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, where he drove out the Spanish rulers with the support of the local peasants who revolted against the landlords.
  5. In 1861 Victor Emmanuel II was proclaimed King of united Italy (Rome joined in 1870); the democratic republic that Mazzini had wanted was never created — the peasants and the poor were left out.
✍️ Answer points — Unification of Britain (3M)
  1. Britain was not formed by a sudden revolution but through a long-drawn-out process; before the eighteenth century the primary identities of the people were ethnic — English, Welsh, Scot or Irish.
  2. The Act of Union (1707) between England and Scotland formed the United Kingdom of Great Britain; the British parliament was henceforth dominated by England.
  3. The English nation was forged through common symbols and institutions — the Union Jack, the national anthem, the Reformation, and the exclusion of Catholics from Parliament in 1701 — while the Welsh, Scots and Irish were suppressed and subjected to the dominion of the English.
✍️ Answer points — French Revolution & collective identity (3–5M)
  1. The revolution of 1789 transferred sovereignty from the monarchy to a body of French citizens — the Estates General became the National Assembly.
  2. A centralised administrative system made uniform laws and a uniform system of weights and measures; internal customs duties and dues were abolished.
  3. New national symbols were adopted — the tricolour flag, a new hymn (the Marseillaise), a new form of oath, and a centralised administration — and French replaced the regional dialects as the national language.
  4. The ideas of la Patrie (the fatherland) and le Citoyen (the citizen) were popularised through print, pamphlets and newspapers, the Jacobin clubs, and education.

✍️ Answer points — Impact of the First World War (5M)
  1. The war created a new economic and political situation: the defence expenditure of the government was financed by war loans and by raising custom duties and introducing income tax.
  2. Forced recruitment in the rural areas for the British Indian Army caused widespread resentment; crop failure in 1918–19 led to acute shortages of food.
  3. An influenza epidemic ravaged the country, and prices roughly doubled between 1913 and 1918 — the poor, the soldiers, and the small zamindars and landlords suffered the most.
  4. The government passed the Defence of India Act and then the Rowlatt Act (1919), allowing detention without trial; Gandhiji called for a hartal on 6 April 1919 (the Rowlatt Satyagraha).
  5. The atmosphere of repression culminated in the Jallianwala Bagh massacre, and the nationalist movement turned from a limited one into a mass movement.
✍️ Answer points — Jallianwala Bagh (3–5M)
  1. On 13 April 1919, a crowd of peasants, workers and city-dwellers gathered in the enclosed ground of Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar, Punjab — a bagh with a narrow entrance, high walls and only one exit — to protest against the arrest of Dr Saifuddin Kitchlew and Dr Satyapal and to celebrate Baisakhi.
  2. Although the announcement relaxing the ban on public meetings (from 10 pm to 10.30 pm) never reached the people, General Dyer entered with armed troops, blocked the only exit and ordered his men to fire without warning.
  3. Hundreds were killed and thousands wounded; people jumped into a well in the compound to escape the bullets.
  4. The nation was shocked: Rabindranath Tagore renounced his knighthood, and Gandhiji returned the Kaisar-i-Hind and other honours he had received.
  5. Nationalist feelings ran very high, particularly in Punjab, and the incident became the immediate backdrop to the Non-Cooperation Movement.
✍️ Answer points — Participants in the Non-Cooperation Movement (5M)
  1. The middle classes in the cities: the movement began with middle-class participation — students, teachers and lawyers; people surrendered titles and boycotted the civil services, the army, the police, the courts and the legislative councils, schools and foreign goods.
  2. Peasants: in Awadh the peasant movement led by Baba Ramchandra was against the talukdars and landlords — demanding reduction of revenue, abolition of begar and social boycott of oppressive landlords; the Eka movement demanded commutation of rent; in Punjab the Akali agitation; elsewhere the Kisan Sabhas.
  3. Plantation workers in Assam: under the Inland Emigration Act, 1859 they were not permitted to leave the tea gardens without permission; for them Swaraj meant the right to move freely in and out of the tea gardens and a raise in their wages.
  4. Workers: the All India Trade Union Congress was founded in 1920; there were strikes in 1921 including the Buckingham and Carnatic Mills strike in Madras and strikes on the Assam-Bengal railways and in the Tata Iron and Steel Company.
  5. The movement was called off in February 1922 after the Chauri Chaura incident in Gorakhpur, where a peaceful demonstration turned violent and 22 policemen were burnt alive.
✍️ Answer points — Civil Disobedience Movement: who participated (5M)
  1. Rich peasants — the Patidars of Gujarat and the Jats of Uttar Pradesh — were active; they resented the high revenue demand and the colonial government's refusal to reduce it when prices collapsed.
  2. Poor peasants were interested in the movement but wanted the unpaid rent owed to the landlords to be remitted; they were kept out of the movement by their landlords, most of whom were Congressmen.
  3. Business classes and industrialists reacted against colonial policies that restricted business; they formed the FICCI in 1927 and attacked colonial control over the Indian economy.
  4. The industrial working class participated in limited numbers — the Nagpur workers took part in the Salt Satyagraha, Sholapur mill workers observed a hartal in 1930, and the Chotanagpur tinplate workers went on strike.
  5. Women participated in large numbers in the salt satyagraha and in picketing foreign cloth and liquor shops; Muslim participation was limited — after the decline of the Non-Cooperation-Khilafat movement a large section felt alienated, and the Congress, fearing to offend the orthodox Hindus, could not fully support the Khilafat cause.

✍️ Answer points — Silk Route (3M)
  1. The Silk Routes were a network of overland and maritime trade routes that linked Asia with Europe and northern Africa, named after the Chinese silk carried along them.
  2. They were in use from before the Christian era and flourished for many centuries, connecting China and Central Asia with Persia, the Mediterranean ports and Rome.
  3. The routes carried more than goods: merchants, pilgrims, missionaries, soldiers and nomads travelled along them, and with them travelled ideas, technologies, religions (Buddhism, Islam, Christianity) and food items — noodles and pasta travelled from China westwards.
  4. An important spin-off was the spread of disease: the bubonic plague travelled westwards along the same routes and devastated Europe in the fourteenth century.
✍️ Answer points — Disease and the conquest of America (3–5M)
  1. America had been cut off from the rest of the world for millennia, so its people had no immunity to the diseases that were common in Europe and Africa — above all smallpox.
  2. When the Spanish arrived (Cortés conquered the Aztecs in 1519; Pizarro the Incas in 1535), they carried these germs with them; smallpox, measles, typhus and influenza spread faster than the soldiers and killed entire communities.
  3. The native population collapsed — in places by up to 90% — destroying the social fabric, the armies and the ability to resist; the conquest was therefore a biological conquest as much as a military one.
  4. Because the European colonisers could not work in the mines and plantations, they turned to the African slave trade to replace the lost labour force.
  5. Line for the examiner: the Spanish invaded “not with guns alone, but with germs”.
✍️ Answer points — Rinderpest in Africa & the 19th century (3M)
  1. Rinderpest (cattle plague) arrived in Africa in the late 1880s, carried by infected cattle imported from British Asia to feed the Italian soldiers invading Eritrea.
  2. It killed about 90% of the cattle in southern and eastern Africa within a few years, destroying the livelihoods of African pastoralists.
  3. The loss of cattle forced Africans into the labour market and into European hands, so the rinderpest helped the colonisers conquer and control Africa.
  4. Other 19th-century drivers: railways, steamships and the telegraph; the abolition of the Corn Laws in Britain leading to cheap grain imports; and indentured labour migration from India to the Caribbean, Fiji, Mauritius, South Africa and the tea gardens of Assam.

✍️ Answer points — Impact of print in Europe / technological innovations (3–5M)
  1. From hand printing to mechanical printing: the hand printing press gave way to the mechanical press; production multiplied many times over and the cost of each book fell sharply.
  2. Woodblock and metal type: China used woodblock printing (the Diamond Sutra, AD 868); Korea and Japan developed metal type; in Europe Gutenberg's metal-type press (1448) made mass production possible.
  3. Improvements to the press itself: the power-driven cylindrical press, the steam-powered rotary press and later the offset press allowed thousands of copies to be printed quickly and cheaply.
  4. Cheap paper and better ink: new paper-making machinery and later the use of wood pulp made paper affordable, so books, newspapers and periodicals came within the reach of ordinary people.
  5. Distribution networks: railways and steamships carried printed material across countries and the telegraph carried the news, creating a mass reading public — the “reading mania” — and spreading the ideas of the Reformation, the Enlightenment and the French Revolution.

✍️ Answer points — Belgium vs Sri Lanka (5M)
  1. Belgium's ethnic composition: 59% of the total population lives in the Flemish region and speaks Dutch; another 40% lives in Wallonia and speaks French; the remaining 1% speaks German. In the capital city Brussels, 80% of the people speak French while 20% are Dutch-speaking.
  2. Belgium's problem: the minority French-speaking community was relatively rich and powerful, and got special protection at the time of independence in 1830; this was resented by the Dutch-speaking majority, and the tension between the two communities came to a head in the 1960s.
  3. Belgium's solution: between 1970 and 1993 the constitution was amended four times — the number of Dutch and French-speaking ministers is equal in the central government; many powers of the central government were given to the state governments of the two regions, which are not subordinate to the central government; and Brussels has a separate government in which both communities have equal representation.
  4. A third kind of government — the community government — is elected by the people belonging to the Dutch, French and German-speaking communities; it has the power regarding cultural, educational and language-related issues.
  5. Sri Lanka's contrast: 74% Sinhala-speaking and 18% Tamil-speaking (13% Sri Lankan Tamils in the north and east, and 5% Indian Tamils — the plantation workers); by religion, 82% Buddhist, 8% Muslim and 8% Christian. After independence in 1948, majoritarian measures followed: Sinhala was made the sole official language in 1956, the government followed policies favouring Sinhala applicants for university positions and government jobs, and Buddhism was given the foremost place. The Tamils felt discriminated against, and the distrust led to a civil war — the LTTE launched a war in 1983.
✍️ Answer points — Why power sharing is desirable (3–5M)
  1. Prudential reason (i): power sharing helps to reduce the possibility of conflict between social groups; social conflict often leads to violence and political instability.
  2. Prudential reason (ii): it ensures the stability of the political order; imposing the will of the majority community over others may look like an attractive option in the short run, but in the long run it undermines the unity of the nation.
  3. Moral reason (i): power sharing is the very spirit of democracy; a democratic rule involves sharing power with those affected by its exercise.
  4. Moral reason (ii): a legitimate government is one where citizens, through participation, acquire a stake in the system — people have a right to be consulted on how they are to be governed.
  5. Conclusion line: the prudent and the moral reasons cannot be separated — the prudent reason is based on a careful calculation of gains and losses, while the moral reason upholds the spirit of democracy.
✍️ Answer points — Forms of power sharing (5M)
  1. Horizontal distribution — among different organs of government: power is shared among the legislature, executive and judiciary, so that no organ can exercise unlimited power; each organ can check the others — this is called a system of checks and balances.
  2. Vertical division — among governments at different levels: power is divided between a general government for the entire country and governments at the provincial or regional level — in India, the Union government, the State governments, and the local self-government (the panchayats and the municipalities).
  3. Among different social groups: power may also be shared among different social groups such as the linguistic and religious groups — in Belgium by the community government; in India by the reservations for the SCs, STs and OBCs and the one-third reservation for women in local government.
  4. Among political parties, pressure groups and movements: in a democracy, the political parties contest elections and share power, and the pressure groups and movements control or influence those in power and thus share it.
  5. Key idea to remember: in a democracy, political expression of majority rule means that in every election or decision different persons and groups may and can form a majority — the majority and the minority opinions are not permanent.
Definitions to write in one line each:
· Majoritarianism — a belief that the majority community should be able to rule a country in whichever way it wants, disregarding the wishes and needs of the minority.
· Community government — a government in Belgium elected by the people belonging to one language community (Dutch, French or German-speaking), with powers over cultural, educational and language-related issues.
· Civil war — a violent conflict between opposing groups within a country.
· Prudential — based on prudence, that is, on a careful calculation of gains and losses.

✍️ Answer points — Key features of federalism (5M)
  1. There are two or more levels (tiers) of government.
  2. Different tiers of government govern the same citizens, but each tier has its own jurisdiction in specific matters of legislation, taxation and administration.
  3. The jurisdictions of the respective levels or tiers of government are specified and guaranteed by the Constitution.
  4. The fundamental provisions of the Constitution cannot be unilaterally changed by one level of government — such changes require the consent of both levels of government.
  5. Courts have the power to interpret the Constitution and the powers of the different levels of government; the highest court acts as an umpire if any dispute arises between them.
  6. Sources of revenue for each level are clearly specified to ensure financial autonomy.
  7. The federal system thus has dual objectives: to safeguard and promote the unity of the country, while at the same time accommodating regional diversity.
✍️ Answer points — Three Lists & the 1992 amendment (3–5M)
  1. Union List (97 subjects) — subjects of national importance such as defence, foreign affairs, banking, communications and currency; only the Union government can make laws on these.
  2. State List (66 subjects) — subjects of State and local importance such as police, trade, commerce, agriculture and irrigation; normally the State governments alone can make laws on them.
  3. Concurrent List (47 subjects) — subjects of common interest such as education, forest, trade unions, marriage, adoption and succession; both the Union and the State governments can make laws, and in case of a conflict the Union law prevails.
  4. Residuary subjects — subjects that do not fall in any of the three lists are dealt with by the Union government.
  5. The 1992 amendment: the Constitution (73rd and 74th) Amendment Acts, 1992 made the third tier of government more powerful and effective — it is now constitutionally mandatory to hold regular elections to local government bodies; seats are reserved for the SCs, STs, OBCs and one-third for women; a State Election Commission has been created; and the gram sabha and the ward committees have been given powers.
MCQ drill (guaranteed): which subject goes to which list — defence / foreign affairs / banking / currency → Union; police / agriculture / irrigation / trade → State; education / forests / trade unions / marriage / adoption / succession → Concurrent. Also: coming together federations = the USA, Switzerland, Australia; holding together federations = India, Spain, Belgium.

✍️ Answer points — Women's oppression & the feminist movement (5M)
  1. Patriarchy produces a sexual division of labour — the household and family work is assigned to women and girls; this work is not paid for and not counted, and it is assumed to be natural and unchangeable.
  2. Consequently women's literacy and participation in paid work remain lower than men's, and the proportion of women in the legislatures is low in India, despite the constitutional guarantee of equality.
  3. First wave of feminism (late 19th–early 20th century): the struggle for political rights — the right to vote, education and property — based on the argument that women are not by birth less capable than men.
  4. Second wave: a broader demand for equal rights, equal wages, the right to divorce and to own property, and for action on domestic violence, dowry and sexual harassment; in India the movement works largely through women's organisations, and the Women's Reservation Bill seeking one-third of the seats for women in the legislatures remains a live demand.
  5. The gains: the more women enter the public sphere, the more they influence politics — raising issues of education, health and employment, and of the burden of unpaid work they carry — and they pressure the political parties to give them a share in power.
✍️ Answer points — Secularism & communalism (3–5M)
  1. Secularism in the Indian Constitution: there is no official religion in India; the Indian state is neither religious nor irreligious — it is non-religious and is not under the control of any religious authority.
  2. Articles 25 to 28 guarantee the freedom of religion to all citizens; the state does not discriminate against anyone on the basis of religion.
  3. The Indian state can intervene in religion-based matters to ensure equality — for example, banning untouchability, abolishing sati, and enacting the Hindu Code Bill which reformed the inheritance rights of women in Hindu society.
  4. But secularism in practice also requires the mutual tolerance of all religions; the followers of a religion cannot be denied the right to profess, practise and propagate their religion.
  5. Communalism takes three forms: (i) communal prejudice and a communal mind-set — the belief that one community's interests are opposed to another's; (ii) communal riots and violence; and (iii) the use of religion in politics — the most common form, using religious symbols, slogans, leaders and sentiments for political mobilisation.
✍️ Answer points — Caste in politics (3–5M)
  1. Caste continues to be a powerful social division in India, and political parties use it to win elections through caste associations and through the selection of candidates.
  2. The political expression of caste inequality has taken several forms — the Dalit movements demanding social justice, and the formation of parties such as the Bahujan Samaj Party (formed in 1984 under the leadership of Kanshi Ram) representing the Dalits and the Bahujans.
  3. But caste inequality is eroding: large-scale urbanisation, the spread of literacy and education, occupational mobility, the weakening of the position of the landlords, and the breaking down of the caste hierarchy through the democratic process have all contributed.
  4. Yet caste has not disappeared from politics: political competition keeps caste identities alive, and voters continue to vote on caste lines; caste has also been used to demand reservation in education and employment.
  5. Conclusion: every social difference does not lead to social division — a social division becomes a political division only when some social difference overlaps with other differences and is perceived as such by the people.

✍️ Answer points — Different people, different goals (5M)
  1. Development is a multi-dimensional concept — what may be development for one may not be development for another; it may even be destructive for the other.
  2. The industrialist vs the fisher-folk: building a dam may bring electricity and industrial development, but it displaces the people and destroys the livelihood of the fisher-folk who depend on the river.
  3. The landless labourer vs the rich farmer: the landless rural labourer wants more days of work and better wages — more income and more equality — while the rich farmer wants higher support prices for his crop and cheaper, more easily available bank credit.
  4. Women within the household: a girl from a rich family may get opportunities similar to her brothers, but a girl from a poor household gets a smaller share of the food and of the family's resources.
  5. Conclusion: people therefore look at a mix of goals — more income, more equality, freedom, security and respect for others — and the developmental goals that are beneficial for one section of people may not be beneficial for another.
✍️ Answer points — World Bank & HDI criteria (5M)
  1. The World Bank uses average income (per capita income) as the criterion for classifying countries in its World Development Report: the countries with a high per capita income are called rich countries and those with a low per capita income are called low-income countries; India falls in the middle-income (low-middle-income) category.
  2. Average income is the total income of the country divided by its total population — a useful but incomplete measure, because it does not tell us how the income is distributed among the people.
  3. The UNDP publishes the Human Development Report, which ranks the countries on the basis of the Human Development Index (HDI).
  4. The HDI uses three inter-related criteria — education, health and income — so it captures the well-being of the people rather than just their income.
  5. Other criteria people use: a mix of goals — more income, more equal distribution, freedom, security and respect — and the report also draws attention to the sustainability of development and to public facilities such as schools, health centres and safe drinking water.

✍️ Answer points — Why did the tertiary sector rise? (5M) — the example he uses for Hack #1
  1. In any country several services such as hospitals, educational institutions, post and telegraph services, police stations, courts, defence, transport and banks are required — these can be called the basic services, and in a developing country the government has to take the responsibility for their provision.
  2. The development of agriculture and industry leads to the development of services such as transport, trade and storage; the greater the development of the primary and the secondary sectors, the greater is the demand for such services.
  3. As income levels rise, certain sections of people start demanding many more services — eating out, tourism, shopping, private hospitals, private schools and professional training centres.
  4. Over the past ten or fifteen years certain new services, such as those based on information and communication technology, have become important and essential.
  5. Point to remember for the marking scheme: the share of the tertiary sector in the GDP has increased the most, while the share of the primary sector has declined — but the employment generated by the tertiary sector has not risen in the same proportion as its share in the GDP.
✍️ Answer points — Organised vs unorganised sector (3M + case-based)
  1. Definition: the organised sector covers those enterprises or places of work where the terms of employment are regular and therefore the people have assured work; the unorganised sector is characterised by small and scattered units which are largely outside the control of the government.
  2. Rules and registration: the organised sector is registered by the government and has to follow the rules and regulations given in various laws such as the Factories Act, the Minimum Wages Act, the Payment of Gratuity Act and the Shops and Establishments Act; in the unorganised sector there are no rules and regulations that are followed.
  3. Job security and benefits: workers in the organised sector enjoy security of employment, work only a fixed number of hours, and get benefits such as the provident fund, gratuity and medical facilities. In the unorganised sector the jobs are low-paid and often not regular, there is no provision for overtime, paid leave, holidays or leave due to sickness, and the employment is subject to a high degree of insecurity.
✍️ Answer points — Creating more employment & the public sector (3–5M)
  1. The problem: a large number of people in India are employed in the primary sector, and there is underemployment (disguised unemployment) — people appear to be employed but all of them are made to work less than their potential, which means that more people are employed than are really needed.
  2. Measures to create more employment: (i) identify, promote and locate industries and services in the semi-rural areas where a large number of people may be employed; (ii) open more service-sector enterprises such as a school, a training centre or a small hospital in the semi-rural areas; (iii) invest in the basic agricultural infrastructure such as a canal or a tube-well; (iv) government spending on the construction of roads, railway lines and canals; (v) provide credit and support to the self-employed and to the small-scale industries, and work through schemes such as the MGNREGA.
  3. Why the public sector still matters: the purpose of the public sector is not just to earn profits. The government owns most of the assets and provides the services — it raises money through taxes and other revenues to pay for the services which it provides to the people.
  4. The public sector provides the basic facilities — education, health, food and security — to the people, and it is the duty of the government to provide these facilities at an affordable cost.
  5. The activities of the private sector are guided by the motive to earn profits; several things that the public sector does — such as building roads, generating electricity, and providing health and education to the poor — are needed by the people but are not profitable for the private sector.

✍️ Answer points — Loan activities of banks (3–5M)
  1. Banks accept deposits from the public and pay an interest on them; the deposits are of two types — the demand deposits, which can be withdrawn on demand, and the time deposits, which are repayable only after a fixed period.
  2. Banks keep only a small proportion of their deposits as cash with themselves (about 15%) — this is kept as provision to pay the depositors who might come to withdraw money from the bank on any given day.
  3. Banks use the major portion of the deposits to extend loans — they are able to lend because only a fraction of the depositors come to withdraw cash on any particular day.
  4. The banks charge a higher rate of interest on the loans than what they offer on the deposits, and the difference between the two rates of interest is their main source of income.
  5. The RBI supervises the functioning of the formal sources of loans: it monitors the banks in actually maintaining the cash balance, and it sees that the banks give loans not just to the profit-making businesses and the traders but also to the small cultivators, the small-scale industries and to the small borrowers.
✍️ Answer points — Self Help Groups (5M) — the topic he says always comes
  1. What they are: a Self Help Group is a small group of about 20 people — usually poor women from the same neighbourhood or village — who pool their savings and meet regularly to save and to lend among themselves.
  2. How they work: the group collects a small amount from each member every month, maintains a common fund, and gives loans to the members at a low rate of interest for their needs — to buy seeds or a sewing machine, to start a small business, or to meet an emergency.
  3. Why they matter — collateral: the biggest advantage is that they help the borrowers overcome the problem of the lack of collateral. The group stands as a guarantee for the loan, and the regular repayments build a record that makes the group eligible for a bank loan.
  4. Why they matter — the bank link: once the group is able to convince the bank about its credit-worthiness, the bank lends to the whole group without any collateral, and the members decide the purpose of the loan and its repayment — this is the micro-credit programme.
  5. Importance: the SHGs are the building blocks of the organisation of the rural poor; they help women to become financially self-reliant, to access cheap and affordable credit, to escape the clutches of the moneylender, and to gain the self-confidence to participate in decision-making — there are estimated to be over a million SHGs working in India, and they have also helped in the empowerment of women.
✍️ Answer points — Why cheap and affordable credit matters (3–5M)
  1. Credit plays a vital and positive role in the development of the country: it helps people from all walks of life to set up their business, to increase their income and to improve their living conditions.
  2. Crop production involves significant costs — the farmers need credit to buy the seeds, the fertilisers, the pesticides and the water, and to repair their farm implements and to buy a tractor.
  3. Cheap and affordable credit helps the small farmers to tide over crop failure caused by natural disasters such as drought or flood, and it keeps them from being caught in a debt trap.
  4. The formal sector credit is much cheaper than the informal sector — the moneylenders charge a very high rate of interest, and there is no organisation which supervises the credit activities of the lenders in the informal sector. The higher cost of borrowing means that a larger part of the earnings of the borrower is used to repay the loan.
  5. Therefore, the formal sources of credit need to expand, and cheap and affordable credit needs to be made available to the people in the rural areas — it is the responsibility of the RBI and the government to monitor this and to ensure that it happens.
Definitions in one line each:
· Barter system — a system in which goods are directly exchanged without the use of money.
· Double coincidence of wants — what a person desires to sell is exactly what the other person wishes to buy; money removes this requirement by acting as a medium of exchange.
· Collateral — an asset that the borrower owns and uses as a guarantee to a lender until the loan is repaid.
· Terms of credit — the interest rate, the collateral and documentation requirement, and the mode of repayment, which together vary from one credit arrangement to another.

✍️ Answer points — Definition & the two enabling factors (3M)
  1. Definition: globalisation is the process of rapid integration or interconnection between countries — mostly through the flow of goods, services, investment, technology and ideas across the borders.
  2. Factor 1 — technology: the rapid improvement in transport technology (container ships, air freight) and in information and communication technology (the telephone, the internet, satellites) has made the movement of goods and the transmission of information across the world much faster and cheaper.
  3. Factor 2 — liberalisation: the removal of the barriers or the restrictions set up by the government on foreign trade and foreign investment, which began in India in 1991, has allowed the MNCs to set up factories and offices and to invest in the country.
  4. Effect: MNCs set up production jointly with the local companies, and they exercise control over production through the joint production, the buying of the local companies, and the placing of orders for the small producers — this has led to greater choice and lower prices for the consumers, but the benefits of globalisation have not been shared equally.

The essentials, if it is in your syllabus

  • The consumer movement in India and the need for consumer awareness — the COPRA (Consumer Protection Act, 1986), and the consumer courts at the district, state and national levels.
